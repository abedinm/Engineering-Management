## Competitiveness, operations & productivity

- **Competitiveness:** an organisation's ability to deliver products or services *more effectively and efficiently than rivals*. It usually comes down to **cost, quality, speed and innovation**.
- Firms differentiate on strategy, product/service, marketing, **operations**, distribution and R&D. The slide marks operations as the **#1 reason most organisations fail**.
- **Operational strategy** turns inputs into outputs through the 10 decisions from lecture 1: design, cost, location, quality, quick response, flexibility, inventory, supply chain, service quality, and managers & workers.
- The **engineering manager's main job** is to make productive use of the organisation's resources, measured by how *efficiently* units are produced.

> High productivity → cost leadership, faster delivery, better margins → **better competitiveness**

- The three factors critical to **productivity improvement** are **labour, capital and management**.

## Calculating productivity

$$\text{Productivity} = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Input}}$$

Output means goods or services. Input means labour, materials, energy and other resources.

**Measurement problems:**
1. Quality may change while quantity stays the same.
2. External factors may interfere.
3. Precise units of measure may be missing.

$$\text{Productivity growth} = \frac{\text{Current} - \text{Previous}}{\text{Previous}} \times 100\%$$

Example: productivity rises from 80 to 84 → (84 − 80) / 80 × 100 = **5%**.

Productivity growth drives a country's inflation rate and its standard of living.

## Why productivity matters

- It tracks performance over time. When productivity slips, you examine the factors in the ratio to find what changed and fix it.
- Rising productivity adds value to the economy while keeping inflation in check.
- **Efficient** means doing the job well with minimum resources and waste (doing things right). **Effective** means doing the right thing (the right strategy).
- It matters most for a **low-cost strategy**: the higher the productivity, the lower the cost per unit.
- A productivity ratio can be computed for a single operation, a department, an organisation or a whole country. Managers use it to plan workforce needs, schedule equipment and do financial analysis.

## Process yield

A measure closely related to productivity.

- **Products:** good output (defects excluded) ÷ raw material input.
- **Services:** depends on the process. For example:
  - Car rental: cars rented ÷ cars available that day.
  - University admissions: acceptances ÷ students approved for admission.
  - Subscriptions: new subscriptions ÷ calls made or letters mailed.

## Productivity factors

**Main factors:** methods, capital, quality, technology and management.

| Factor | Effect on productivity |
|---|---|
| Standardising processes and procedures | **+** Reduces variability, which helps productivity and quality |
| Quality differences | Distort measurements, e.g. a factory today vs 30 years ago |
| Use of the internet | **+** Lowers transaction costs |
| Searching for lost or misplaced items | **−** Wastes time |
| Scrap rates | **−** Signal wasted resources |
| New workers | **−** Lower output at first, so growing firms see a productivity lag |
| Safety | **−** Accidents cost productivity |
| Shortage of tech-savvy workers | **−** Limits upgrades and growth |
| Layoffs | **±** Short-term rise (same work, fewer people), then burnout, fear of more cuts, and the best people leaving |
| Labour turnover | **−** Replacements need time to get up to speed |
| Workspace design | **+** Tools within easy reach |
| Incentive plans | **+** Reward productivity gains. Higher productivity is also an alternative to outsourcing |

The best organisations aim for **continuous improvement**, and productivity gains are part of that.

## Improving productivity: steps

1. Develop productivity measures.
2. Find the critical (**bottleneck**) operations.
3. Develop methods for improvement.
4. Set reasonable goals.
5. Get management support.
6. Measure and publicise the improvements.

**Case: Toyota Kaizen.** Kaizen means continuous incremental improvement. Daily team huddles produce 500+ small upgrades a month. The result was **4.2% average annual productivity growth** from 2010 to 2023.

## There are several routes to competitiveness

- **Samsung vs TSMC (semiconductors):** Samsung went for cost leadership through labour productivity, getting 12% more chips per wafer than the industry average. TSMC competed on innovation and pioneered 3 nm chips. **TSMC won 54% market share despite Samsung's productivity edge.** Productivity alone isn't enough.
- **Apple vs Samsung (electronics):** Apple's MFP is higher because of design reuse and tighter integration across functions.

## Single-factor vs multifactor vs total productivity

| Type | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Partial (single-factor)** | Output ÷ Labour, Output ÷ Machine, Output ÷ Capital, Output ÷ Energy |
| **Multifactor** | Output ÷ (Labour + Machine), Output ÷ (Labour + Capital + Energy) |
| **Total** | All goods/services produced ÷ All inputs used |

**All inputs in the denominator must be in the same unit**, usually dollars. Partial measures are the most useful in operations management:

| Partial measure | Examples |
|---|---|
| Labour | Units per labour-hour, units per shift, value added per labour-hour, $ output per labour-hour |
| Machine | Units per machine-hour, $ output per machine-hour |
| Capital | Units per $ input, $ output per $ input |
| Energy | Units per kWh, $ output per kWh |

> **Watch the units.** Check whether figures are daily, weekly or yearly, and convert everything to the same basis before dividing.

## Worked examples from the slides

| Problem | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 4 workers install 720 sq yd of carpet in 8 h | 720 / (4 × 8) = 720 / 32 | **22.5 yd per labour-hour** |
| Machine makes 70 pieces in 2 h, 2 unusable | (70 − 2) / 2 | **34 pieces/hour** (count usable output only) |
| 7,040 units; labour $1,000, materials $520, overhead $2,000 | 7,040 / 3,520 | **2 units per $** |
| 400 cases in ½ h with 4 workers | 400 / (4 × 0.5) | **200 cases per labour-hour** |
| 2,000 rolls; labour $160, material $50, overhead $320 | 2,000 / 530 | **3.77 rolls per $** |
| 300 usable units, 3 workers × 8 h at $20/h, 600 lb at $1/lb, overhead = 1.5 × labour | 300 / (480 + 600 + 720) = 300 / 1,800 | **0.167 units per $** |
| Health club: 2 staff × 40 h at $20/h, 400 leads each, 10% convert at $100; material $130, overhead $1,000 per week | (400 × 2 × 100 × 0.10) / (1,600 + 130 + 1,000) = 8,000 / 2,730 | **2.93 $ fees per $ input** |

If the output is in dollars (units × price), the MFP is "revenue per $ input".

## Class practice: solutions

### Practice 1: smartwatch maker

**Given:** 20 workers per shift, 2 shifts × 8 h, 5 days/week, 48 weeks/year, wage $10/h. For 2022: material $30,000, energy $10,000, overhead = 20% of labour cost. 1,000 watches made, 5% rejected, price $100.

**(a) MFP for 2022, as revenue per $ input**

- Labour-hours = 20 × 2 × 8 × 5 × 48 = **76,800 h**
- Labour cost = 76,800 × $10 = **$768,000**
- Overhead = 0.20 × 768,000 = **$153,600**
- Total input = 768,000 + 153,600 + 30,000 + 10,000 = **$961,600**
- Sellable output = 1,000 × 0.95 = 950 watches → revenue = 950 × $100 = **$95,000**

$$\text{MFP}_{2022} = \frac{95{,}000}{961{,}600} \approx \mathbf{0.0988}\ \text{\$ revenue per \$ input}$$

**(b) 2023: $2,000 investment brings quality to 100% with the same output. Does MFP improve?**

- Sellable output = 1,000 → revenue = **$100,000**
- Input = 961,600 + 2,000 = **$963,600**

$$\text{MFP}_{2023} = \frac{100{,}000}{963{,}600} \approx \mathbf{0.1038}$$

Growth = (0.1038 − 0.0988) / 0.0988 ≈ **+5.0%**. **Yes, MFP improves.** The investment adds only 0.2% to input costs, while 5% more of the output becomes sellable. Revenue rises by $5,000 for a $2,000 outlay, so the investment is worth making.

### Practice 2: Modern Lumber (Art Binley)

**(a) Labour productivity, crates per labour-hour**

- Now: 100 logs × 3 h = 300 labour-hours → 240 / 300 = **0.800 crates/LH**
- With the buyer: 300 + 8 = 308 labour-hours → 260 / 308 = **0.844 crates/LH**
- Change = (0.844 − 0.800) / 0.800 ≈ **+5.5%**

**(b) Multifactor productivity, crates per $**

| | Now | With buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Labour | 300 h × $10 = $3,000 | 308 h × $10 = $3,080 |
| Capital | $350 | $350 |
| Energy | $150 | $150 |
| Materials | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| **Total input** | **$4,500** | **$4,580** |
| Output | 240 crates | 260 crates |
| **MFP** | 240 / 4,500 = **0.0533** | 260 / 4,580 = **0.0568** |

MFP growth = (0.0568 − 0.0533) / 0.0533 ≈ **+6.4%**. Hiring the buyer improves both labour productivity and MFP.

## In-review MCQs: answers

| Q | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Productivity increases when… | **(b)** inputs decrease while outputs stay the same | Smaller denominator, same numerator |
| 2. Services often… | **(c)** are knowledge-based | Services are intangible, non-standard and high-contact |
| 3. Productivity… | **(d)** depends on labour, management and capital | The three factors on slide 3 |
| 4. Single-factor productivity… | **(c)** usually uses labour as the factor | Labour productivity is the most common partial measure |
| 5. Multifactor productivity… | **(c)** usually uses substitutes as common variables for the factors of production | Usually dollars, so different inputs can be added together |

## Likely exam points

- Productivity formula, growth % formula, process yield.
- Efficient vs effective.
- Partial vs multifactor vs total productivity, and why the denominator needs a common unit.
- **Numericals:** labour productivity, MFP in units/$ or revenue/$, before-and-after comparisons with % change. Count only good units, and convert everything to the same time basis.
- Productivity factors, especially the layoffs paradox and the new-worker lag.
- The six steps to improve productivity, and the Toyota Kaizen and Samsung vs TSMC cases.
